For forty years, Elias had kept the lighthouse on the rocky island at the edge of the bay. Every evening, as the sun disappeared behind the hills, he climbed the one hundred and twelve steps to the top of the tower and lit the great lamp. Every morning, he climbed them again to put it out. He had never missed a single night.

The island was small and quiet. Apart from Elias, the only residents were a family of seabirds and an old grey cat that had arrived on a fishing boat years before and simply decided to stay. Once a month, a boat from the village brought him food, letters and newspapers. Elias did not mind the solitude. He liked the sound of the wind, and he believed that his work mattered.

One autumn afternoon, a letter arrived that changed everything. The government had decided to replace the old lamp with an automatic light. It would turn on and off by itself, and no keeper would be needed. Elias would have to leave the island by the end of the year.

He read the letter three times. Then he folded it carefully and put it in his pocket. That night, as he climbed the stairs, the steps seemed longer than ever.

In the weeks that followed, he tried to imagine his new life in the village. He would have neighbors, a small apartment, and nothing to do in the evenings. He was not sure whether he felt relieved or afraid.

Then, in early December, a terrible storm hit the coast. The wind was so strong that it broke the electric cables that connected the island to the mainland. The new automatic system, which had been installed only a few days earlier, went dark.

Elias did not hesitate. He took the old oil lamp out of the storage room, cleaned it as quickly as he could, and carried it up the tower. His hands were shaking from the cold, but after several attempts, the flame caught. A warm light began to turn slowly over the black sea.

Far away, a small fishing boat was fighting against the waves. Its captain had lost his way in the rain and was heading straight toward the rocks. Suddenly, he saw the light. He turned the wheel just in time.

The next morning, the captain came to the island to thank the keeper. He brought his two young sons with him. The boys had never seen a lighthouse from the inside, so Elias showed them the stairs, the lamp and the enormous lens that made the light so powerful.

"Will you teach us how it works?" the older boy asked.

Elias smiled for the first time in weeks. The automatic light would be repaired, and he would still have to leave the island. But he understood now that some things should not be forgotten. In the spring, he started visiting the village school every Friday, telling the children stories about the sea, the storms and the lights that guide people home.
